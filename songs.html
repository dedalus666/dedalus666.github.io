I

Sky Woman

The Story

Native Iroquois legend tells of a great island floating high in the sky amongst the clouds, where the Sky People lived long before the world that we know existed. There was no death among them, no sadness — only an eternal luminous peace.

But Sky Woman was with child — carrying twin sons — and her husband, consumed by jealousy and rage, tore out the great light-giving tree at the center of their island and pushed her through the hole into the void below. Two great birds caught her falling form and carried her down toward the dark waters of the primordial earth.

There, the animals gathered. A great turtle offered the shell of its back. Others dove to the seafloor to bring up mud, which they spread upon the turtle's shell, and from that small beginning, the earth was made. Sky Woman stepped onto that land and created the moon, the sun, and the stars. She gave birth to twin sons — Sapling, the gentle creator of beaches and rainbows, and Flint, the agent of chaos, who made winter, volcanoes, and poison. The Great Spirit was pleased with their work. The final gift she gave to humanity was the freedom to dream — not foreseeing what they would do with it.

II

Curse of Jaxx!

The Story

There was a woman in the town known as Jaxx! — the exclamation mark hers by declaration. She carried books and card tricks hidden in her skirts, and the rumor of the town was that she had studied under a White Witch, whose body she had found dead in the bog. She was not like the others. The townspeople feared what they didn't understand.

The White Witch was murdered. And when Jaxx! learned of it — whether in grief, in love, or in the rage of a daughter who has lost her mother — she plunged a knife and then herself into a well. Her body was never recovered.

But the curse she released in that moment could be felt in the Blood-Moon light. The town's ignorance had cost it everything. The towers of truth crumbled. Peace, happiness, and youth were taken from them. And to the elders who remained, a lesson was passed with great solemnity: save your judgment for the one behind the cold, steel glass — the mirror that shows only yourself.

III

Rosabelle Believe

The Story

"Rosabelle Believe" was a secret code — known only to the magician Harry Houdini and his wife Bess. Before his death, Houdini promised his wife he would send her a message from beyond the grave using this phrase, proof that consciousness survives death. For ten years after he died on Halloween 1926, Bess lit a candle for him every Halloween and waited. She never received the message. She believed until the day she died.

This song inhabits the space between worlds — the vantage point of Houdini's ghost, desperate to reach her. He can see her. He can feel her grief. He speaks — but the veil does not conduct sound. He touches her face — but matter does not respond to spirit. He loves her with the full force of a man who spent his life mastering the impossible, and the one impossible thing that defeats him is simply this: there may be an afterlife, but there is no possible way to communicate with the living.

The candle burns. She waits. He watches.

IV

Betrayer Moon

The Story

This is a fever dream. There is no single narrative here — only the swirling architecture of a mind in crisis, navigating loss, grief, and the peculiar clarity that can emerge from the depths of delirium.

The moon becomes a traitor. Its cold light, which should be romantic, becomes the illumination of betrayal — cascading down over icy tears and crowns of lies, revealing what daylight had obscured. The dreamer falls off a mountain and cannot shake the light. They are caught in quicksand and watch others clamor for the promise of a thick knotted rope. Shadows become flags. Thumbprints on stained glass become labyrinths.

But somewhere deep in the blackness — as the song moves through its fever — there is a resolve. A strength that was cloaked beneath the madness. Spirits that were silent begin to rise. Prisms bend light into rainbows. The sand becomes a beach of bones, yes — but time has crushed those bones into glass, and glass can still catch light.

V

Curse of Pasiphae

I. The Minstrel's Prophecy  ·  II. Ire of Poseidon  ·  III. Curse and Conception  ·  IV. Poisonous Vengeance  ·  V. Coda — Citadel of Sorrow

The Story

King Minos of Crete was given a white bull by Poseidon — a gift from the god of the sea — with the understanding that it would be sacrificed in his honor. Minos, greedy and proud, sacrificed a lesser animal instead. Poseidon discovered the deception and his rage was absolute.

As punishment, the god cursed not the King, but his Queen — Pasiphae, the immortal goddess of witchcraft and sorcery. The curse ignited a lust so overwhelming and mad that Pasiphae commissioned the great architect Daedalus to build a wooden cow in which she could conceal herself. She lay with the white bull. She was impregnated. And she gave birth to Asterios — the Minotaur, half man, half bull — whom she loved, though the world called him monster.

Daedalus, that same architect, was then charged by Minos to build the great Labyrinth to imprison Asterios forever, wandering its endless halls alone. But the witch-queen's vengeance was not spent. Minos was a faithless and vicious man, and Pasiphae cast upon him a final, annihilating curse: that he would kill each lover he took with his own body. The labyrinth was not only for her son. The labyrinth was for all of them.
